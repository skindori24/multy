A C++ engine does three things here. It bootstraps discount curves from today's market quotes, it prices swaps and bonds off those curves and measures their risk, and it resamples sixty years of yield-curve history (the Crump–Gospodinov bootstrap) to attach honest uncertainty to return forecasts. The page is rebuilt automatically from public end-of-day data.

US dollar curves: SOFR swaps and Treasuries

Two curves are bootstrapped each day: the SOFR curve from par swap rates (the risk-free curve used to discount collateralised derivatives) and the Treasury curve from the government's par yields. Bootstrapping means solving, one maturity at a time, for the discount factors that make every quoted instrument reprice exactly. A zero rate is the yield of a single payment at that maturity; a forward rate is the rate the curve lets you lock in today for a future period.

Market quotes and what the curves imply

A forward-starting swap rate such as 5Y5Y is the 5-year rate, 5 years from now, that today's curve lets you lock in. It is a break-even level, not a forecast.

Swap spread = SOFR swap rate minus the Treasury yield of the same maturity. The z-score compares today's spread with its own recent history; it flags unusual levels, it does not predict a reversal.

Where could yields go? Forecasts with honest uncertainty

Starting from today's Treasury curve, the engine simulates thousands of future curve paths by re-drawing blocks of historical yield-curve "news" (the Crump–Gospodinov bootstrap). The spread of those paths is the uncertainty; nothing here is a point prediction.

Do yield curves tell you the expected return? Only partly. A zero-coupon yield is the return you earn if you hold the bond to maturity. The forward curve is the path of rates at which you would break even, but forwards also contain a risk premium, so on average they have overstated future short rates. That is why the charts below show two lines: what forwards imply, and where history says rates tend to end up.

Replicating the paper: do forward rates predict future rates?

Crump and Gospodinov, Deconstructing the Yield Curve (New York Fed Staff Report 884), show that standard regressions on bond data give misleading p-values, and propose resampling "difference returns" instead of yields. Section 4.2 of the paper tests the expectations hypothesis, under which every slope below would equal 1. The engine re-runs those tests on the Fed's yield history up to the latest complete quarter.

Four ways to run the same test give four different answers

Each panel shows the estimated slope by bond maturity and the range of estimates across bootstrap histories. All panels share one scale, as in Figure 4 of the paper, so the differences in precision are visible: the Campbell–Shiller yield-change test (CS2) is wildly imprecise, the paper's CG test is tight and stable.

p-values are bootstrap p-values (symmetric percentile-t, Newey–West standard errors). A small p-value for "slope = 1" rejects the expectations hypothesis; a small p-value for "slope = 0" says forwards do contain information about future rates.

EUR multicurve: one discount curve, one projection curve per tenor

Since 2008 a 6-month Euribor loan carries more credit and liquidity risk than rolling overnight money, so one curve can no longer do every job. Collateralised swaps are discounted on the overnight (OIS) curve, while each Euribor tenor gets its own projection curve, bootstrapped with OIS discounting held fixed. Free live EUR swap quotes do not exist, so this part runs on a frozen market snapshot (11 December 2012) and is checked against QuantLib.

Moscow Exchange: government bonds against the G-curve

The exchange publishes an official zero-coupon curve for Russian government bonds (the G-curve). Every fixed-coupon OFZ is priced off that curve; the gap between its market price and its curve price, measured as a spread, shows whether the bond is cheap or rich. Because low-coupon and long bonds trade at systematically different spreads, each bond is compared with bonds of similar coupon and duration.

Validation report

A curve engine is tested by checking that it reprices the market and matches trusted references, not by whether its forecasts come true. These checks are recomputed on every run from the same data as the rest of the page.

Method notes

Two different "bootstraps". Curve bootstrapping takes today's quotes and solves for discount factors, with no randomness and no history. Statistical bootstrapping takes a long history of finished curves and resamples it to measure uncertainty. This project does both: the first produces the curves, the second produces the forecast bands and p-values.

What a signal means. Treasury signals compare buying a bond for a year against holding a one-year bill, using the simulated distribution of returns and a regression on the curve's shape. Bond-by-bond signals compare each bond's spread with similar bonds. Both are relative, rule-based and uncertain; the bands on this page are wide because the true uncertainty is wide.

What is not modelled. Intraday prices (all data is end of day), bid-offer costs, funding and repo, taxes, individual US Treasury issues (only the par curve is public), and any guarantee that history repeats.
